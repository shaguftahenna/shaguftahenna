Student Voice & Reflective Evidence

Authenticated student reflections evidencing what learners identified as the most beneficial inclusive changes to the AI module, collected before the Interactive AI Learning Dashboard was introduced, grounded in peer-reviewed literature, and validated through post-intervention survey outcomes in Section 05.

Reflection as evidence of learning

Boud, Keogh and Walker (1985) establish that structured reflection is the cognitive mechanism through which experience becomes learning. Student reflections are therefore not supplementary records, they are the primary evidence that learning has occurred, making them the most academically rigorous form of student voice available to any inclusive educator.

How it is evidenced here

Eight authenticated pre-intervention reflections, organised by module tier, each naming a specific inclusive change students identified as most needed. Nine academic journal connections demonstrate the significance of this student voice as a co-design and reflective evidence mechanism.

Validated post-intervention

Post-intervention survey (MSc, n=15) confirms every student-identified change produced measurable inclusive impact. Post-intervention student reflections from the same cohort, evidencing resolved outcomes, are presented in Section 05 as part of the evaluation evidence.

Before the Dashboard — Traditional Delivery Context

Prior to this intervention, the AI module was delivered through lecture slides, static handouts, and timetabled lab sessions, with no embedded simulation access, no accessibility features, and no structured reflection mechanism.

The reflections below represent authentic diagnostic voice, gathered before the dashboard was introduced, evidencing what learners identified as the changes that would most improve their inclusive access to AI learning. Cook-Sather, Bovill and Felten (2014) establish that this diagnostic voice, gathered before redesign occurs, constitutes the most academically valid basis for inclusive curriculum change.

The Academic Case for Student Voice as Inclusive Co-Design Evidence

Reflection as a learning and evidence mechanism

Boud, Keogh and Walker (1985) establish that structured reflection is not a supplementary activity, it is the cognitive mechanism through which experience becomes learning. Moon (2004) extends this by demonstrating that depth of reflection directly indicates depth of learning, making reflective submissions the most academically robust form of student evidence available. Together, these frameworks position the reflections below not merely as opinions, but as evidence of engagement and metacognitive awareness.

Student voice as co-design resource

Cook-Sather, Bovill and Felten (2014) reframe student voice as a co-design resource rather than an evaluative metric, its value lies in identifying what needs to change before redesign occurs. Brookfield (2017) reinforces this through his student lens framework, arguing that systematically listening to students describe their learning experience is among the most reliable mechanisms for identifying invisible curriculum barriers.

Why formative reflection strengthens self-regulation and inclusion

Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) demonstrate that formative assessment enabling self-regulation produces significantly stronger outcomes, particularly for learners with assessment anxiety. Carless and Boud (2018) show that structured two-stage prompts, i.e., observation then application, develop evaluative judgement more reliably than unguided writing. These findings ground the design of the two-prompt reflection system across all 18 modules: not as a data-collection tool, but as a pedagogically evidenced inclusive intervention.

Student voice as the design brief — Cook-Sather, Bovill and Felten (2014)

Each of the four changes below was named by students in their pre-intervention reflections as the change that would most improve their inclusive experience of the AI module. This is the direct operationalisation of Cook-Sather, Bovill and Felten's (2014) co-design framework. Every change maps to a post-intervention outcome in Section 05.

Cook-Sather et al. (2014) Authenticated student voice Voice-to-design documented Outcomes confirmed Section 05
Embed interactive simulation beyond the timetabled lab
Most frequently named
"The most beneficial change would be to make the simulation available at home, not just in the lab. I need to revisit the moment the algorithm made sense, and right now I cannot do that." — Student 01, pre-intervention

Students consistently identified persistent, self-paced simulation access as the highest-priority inclusive change. Kolb (1984) argues that concrete experience must be revisitable for reflective observation; lab-only access therefore disrupts the reflective cycle. Mayer (2009) further shows that dual-channel learning requires concurrent visual and verbal input. Post-intervention, students reported the embedded simulation as most transformative, consistent across GAN, A*, CNN, and RNN reflections (Section 05).

Kolb (1984) — revisitable concrete experience; Mayer (2009) — dual-channel processing
Addressed: live simulations across all 18 modules, WCAG 2.1 AA · Section 05 confirmed: 100% survey Q6, Q7
Introduce structured reflection prompts after each topic
Most frequently named
"What would help most is something that asks me what I understood and how I would apply it — immediately after the lab, while it's still fresh. Without that, a week later it is gone." — Student 03, pre-intervention

Students identified the absence of guided reflective prompts as a critical barrier to consolidating AI learning. Boud, Keogh and Walker (1985) argue that without deliberate reflection, experiential activity yields surface familiarity rather than conceptual change. Moon (2004) shows that structured two-stage prompts deepen reflection across learners, while Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) demonstrate that low-stakes formative reflection with visible progress feedback strengthens self-regulation. Post-intervention, P.R.’s Transformers reflection and T.N.’s RNN reflection in Section 05 directly evidence this shift.

Boud et al. (1985) — reflection as learning; Moon (2004) — depth taxonomy; Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) — self-regulation
Addressed: two guided reflections per module · Section 05 confirmed: 93.3% survey Q14, Q15
Add persistent accessibility tools
Named: EAL and disability-disclosed students
"The most beneficial change would be a language option and a way to adjust the text, something that stays on every page. That would make the e module feel like it was designed for me." — Student 05, pre-intervention

Students with dyslexia, EAL learners, and those studying outside conventional hours identified persistent, always-visible accessibility tools as the most impactful inclusive change. Rose and Meyer (2002) argue that accessibility designed from the outset produces qualitatively different experiences than retrofitted accommodations. Kieran and Anderson (2018) extend this to linguistic equity, emphasising that UDL must address language diversity alongside disability. Post-intervention, M.L.’s Dijkstra’s reflection and Ch.D.’s Diffusion Models reflection in Section 05 directly confirm the impact of the multi-language selector and read-aloud features.

Rose and Meyer (2002) — UDL architecture; Kieran and Anderson (2018) — linguistic equity; CAST (2024)
Addressed: persistent panel — font scaling, OpenDyslexic, high contrast, read-aloud, EN/ES/FR · S05: 93.3% Q18
Provide multimodal content alongside every lecture
Named across all cohort
"If I could have a video and something interactive alongside the slides, so the concept is shown as well as defined, that would change everything for how I engage with the advanced modules." — Student 01, pre-intervention

Students across cohort named the provision of video, simulation, and written content alongside lecture slides as the change that would improve their access to complex AI concepts. Mayer (2009) demonstrates that dual-channel processing, pairing visual and verbal representations, significantly reduces extraneous cognitive load. Bransford, Brown and Cocking (1999) establish that prior knowledge is activated most effectively through multiple representational formats. Post-intervention, 100% of survey respondents confirmed multimodal delivery enhanced their learning, the highest-rated item in the entire survey, directly evidenced in A.M.'s GANs reflection in Section 05.

Mayer (2009) — multimedia learning; Bransford et al. (1999) — scaffolded environments; CAST (2024)
Addressed: PDF, YouTube video, simulation, written summary on every module page · S05: 100% Q6

Context & Collection

Gathered through end-of-module written feedback, via two guided prompts: (1) what was most difficult to access, and (2) what single change would most improve the module's inclusivity.

Authentication

Each reflection is authenticated with student programme and module context, anonymised in accordance with institutional ethics policy while preserving the integrity of the student voice.
