Inclusive Educator Reflection

A critically examined account of professional transformation, demonstrating how the design of an inclusive AI learning environment across 18 modules, three tiers, and one academic year reshaped my understanding of pedagogical design, human diversity, and the conditions under which meaningful learning becomes possible for every student, regardless of prior experience, language, or disability.

A · Inclusive Educator’s Philosophy

“Diversity is not a complication to be managed within a curriculum, it is the fundamental condition of any genuine learning community. Every student engaging with an AI module brings a distinct cognitive history, language background, disability experience, and relationship with technology. The educator’s task is not to reduce that diversity into a single pathway, but to design learning architectures that actively recognise and respond to it. Universal Design for Learning is not simply a framework I apply to my teaching; it is the epistemological position from which I design.”

Dr. Shagufta Henna · Lecturer in Artificial Intelligence · ATU Ireland · 2026
I · Inclusive design as first principle

From accommodation to inclusive curriculum design

Before September 2025, my understanding of inclusion was accommodation-based—reactive adjustments for students encountering barriers. A CAST UDL Guidelines 3.0 audit of my modules identified 21 accessibility barriers within materials previously considered effective teaching. As Burgstahler (2015) argues, the shift from accommodation to universal design is not a technical improvement but a reconceptualisation of teaching itself. This audit revealed an implicit reliance on an “average learner” model that does not reflect the reality of diverse cohorts. In a context where 34% of students are EAL learners and 18% have disclosed disabilities, Hockings (2010) reframes a central design question that now informs my practice: for whom is learning designed, and who is excluded through default assumptions?

II · Complexity, cognition, and the AI learner

Scaffolding without lowering the ceiling

AI is inherently cognitively demanding. Concepts such as GANs and diffusion processes cannot be simplified; they must be encountered and understood in depth. The design challenge was not to reduce complexity, but to create conditions in which complex content becomes cognitively accessible. Mayer’s (2009) dual-channel theory informed a multimodal design integrating PDFs, video, and interactive simulations. De Jong et al. (2023) show that simulation-first approaches support deeper conceptual understanding, particularly for abstract mathematical content. Tools such as GANLab, the A* visualiser, and Stanford ConvNetJS function as primary cognitive entry points. This reflects inclusive design grounded in Vygotsky’s (1978) Zone of Proximal Development, enabling engagement with complexity through scaffolding.

Mayer (2009)De Jong et al. (2023)Vygotsky (1978)
III · Student voice as epistemic co-design

186 reflections as diagnostic evidence

The 186 reflections across eight modules are not supplementary records; in Boud, Keogh and Walker’s (1985) terms, they constitute the cognitive mechanism through which experience becomes learning. Cook-Sather, Bovill and Felten (2014) extend this, arguing that when students are positioned as co-designers of their learning, they exercise epistemic agency. The four structural changes identified, simulation access, structured reflection, language support, and co-design mechanisms, were not requests for reduced difficulty, but diagnostic accounts of structural limitations. Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) situate this within formative feedback, emphasising that acting on student voice closes the loop between teaching intention and learning experience. All four changes were implemented.

Boud et al. (1985)Cook-Sather et al. (2020)Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006)
IV · Sustainable Development Goal 4 (SDG 4) and the ethics of inclusion

Learner diversity as design specification

United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 4 (SDG4) calls for inclusive and equitable quality education for all. In an Irish higher education context where 34% of the AI cohort are EAL learners and 18% have disclosed disabilities, SDG 4 is not an abstraction but a design specification. The multi-language selector, dyslexia font toggle, Web Speech API read-aloud function, and WCAG 2.1 AA compliance are not optional features; they constitute the materialisation of a commitment to what Florian and Black-Hawkins (2011) describe as ordinary inclusive pedagogy. Mezirow’s (1991) theory of transformative learning frames the ethical dimension: genuine inclusion is transformative because it requires the educator to relinquish the assumption of a standardised learner.

01
Professional growth from experience
From content deliverer to learning architect

The most significant professional development arising from this project was not technical but epistemic: it involved a reconstitution of my understanding of the lecturer’s role. Student reflections were the primary mechanism for this shift. Analysis of 186 authenticated submissions revealed the specific conditions under which students were able, or unable, to construct understanding.

Schön (1983) distinguishes between the reflective practitioner, who interrogates practice, and the technical expert, who executes it. For the first decade of my teaching career, I operated predominantly within the latter orientation. Kolb (1984) conceptualises this as active experimentation, where evidence from learner experience is used to iteratively redesign the learning environment.

02
Significantly changed perspectives encompassed and displayed

Three perspectives fundamentally reconstituted by evidence

Mezirow (1991) defines perspective transformation as the restructuring of meaning-making frames through critical reflection. It is not the refinement of existing assumptions but the replacement of one frame with another, resulting in changes in perception, practice, and professional identity. In this project, three of my interpretive frames were restructured through engagement with student evidence, each triggered by specific encounters with learner experience.

Assessment perspective
Written evidence as barrier, not measure

When March 2026 analysis revealed that the students who engaged most deeply with the GANLab had earlier submitted the weakest notes, my assessment frame collapsed. I had been measuring competence through a format that disadvantaged visual-spatial and EAL learners. Schön (1983) names this reflection-on-action: retrospective recognition that the frame itself was generating distorted conclusions about student understanding.

Inclusion perspective
From provision to obligation

Student A’s feedback in March 2026, stating that this was the first AI course in her one-year programme that had not made her feel excluded, was not a compliment but an indictment of the system in which I had been teaching for several years. Hockings (2010) argues that inclusive teaching must be ordinary practice, not an exceptional intervention Her testimony changed permanently how I understand the scale of the obligation.

Student voice perspective
Feedback as epistemic evidence, not preference

I had previously treated student feedback as supplementary. The pre-intervention reflections reconfigured that frame. The four structural gaps they identified were not preferences for reduced difficulty, but evidence of design limitations within the learning environment. Cook-Sather, Bovill and Felten (2014) argue that when students are positioned as co-designers, they generate epistemic evidence of design failure. All four changes were implemented.

Mezirow (1991) Schön (1983) Hockings (2010) Cook-Sather et al. (2014)
03
Complex questions and challenges

Irreducible tensions in inclusive curriculum design

The most intellectually rigorous dimension of this reflection lies in acknowledging what remains unresolved. Boud, Keogh and Walker (1985) distinguish between surface and deep reflection, while Moon (2004) argues that transformative reflection requires sustained engagement with uncertainty rather than the performance of resolution.

Measurement
Submitted ≠ understood

A student may complete both reflections and still experience the AI content as alienating. The two-prompt system cannot guarantee genuine conceptual change; no quantitative metric can fully resolve this gap.

Next step
Asynchronous formative dialogue to complement reflection data with qualitative evidence of understanding.

Scaling
Individual commitment cannot substitute for institutional conditions

Designing 18 accessible modules required sustained investment that the system does not routinely recognise. Florian and Black-Hawkins (2011) argue that for inclusive pedagogy to become practice, it must be supported by institutional structures that are not yet fully established.

This portfolio functions not only as a record of individual practice but also as a form of institutional advocacy.

What the GANLab data revealed about my assessment assumptions
Reviewing reflection submissions, I observed that students who engaged with the GANLab simulation had previously submitted weaker written notes. The simulation altered the form of evidence they were able to produce. This prompted me to question whether my reliance on written notes was functioning as a barrier rather than a valid measure of understanding. Schön (1983) distinguishes between reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action; this instance represents the latter, a retrospective recognition that my assessment lens had been systematically narrow. This observation led to a sustained shift in my approach to assessment.

It shifted my focus from defensiveness to a design question: what would this module look like if designed for all learners from the outset? 7 April 2026
Why I chose UDL as the framework, and what I had to unlearn first
Before September 2025, I understood accessibility primarily through an accommodations model: reasonable adjustments, extended time, and individual support plans. UDL required unlearning this framing entirely. The core shift is from adapting content for students who struggle to designing learning that does not produce avoidable barriers. Burgstahler (2015) describes this as proactive design, while Florian and Black-Hawkins (2011) argue it must become the ordinary condition of teaching. This reorientation replaced additive support structures with a fundamentally different approach to curriculum design.

What postgraduate AI students need that undergraduate provision rarely offers
Postgraduate AI students enter with heterogeneous prior knowledge, competing life demands, and high-stakes trajectories including career transitions, doctoral preparation, and professional upskilling. Blaschke’s (2012) heutagogical model of self-determined learning informed the asynchronous-first design of the dashboard, ensuring persistent, fully accessible content independent of timetabled lab access. With 26% of students engaging between 10pm and 2am, the localStorage system supports continuity of work and ensures that engagement is not constrained.

Key Professional Insight
From accessibility panel data · March 2026

Students who activate at least one accessibility feature in their first session show a 97% module completion rate, compared with 86% for those who do not. Florian and Black-Hawkins (2011) would interpret this as evidence that inclusive design features operate as learning affordances for the entire cohort, rather than as remedial supports for a minority. I now explicitly model the accessibility panel in Week 1, positioning font scaling, dyslexia font, read-aloud, and language selection as standard components.

🔄
Ongoing Challenge
From localStorage timestamp analysis · 2026

26% of students consistently access AI module content between 10pm and 2am. The reflection system ensures submissions are not lost, yet feedback windows and office hours remain structured around 9–5 institutional assumptions. Blaschke (2012) frames this as a heutagogical imperative: self-determined learners require support structures aligned with their actual patterns of engagement. In response, I am trialling asynchronous text-based drop-in sessions as a more equitable alternative, while recognising this as an ongoing and unresolved challenge.

🌿
Next Steps in Practice
Planned for Academic Year 2026–27

Co-design next year’s AI module with a student panel drawn from all three programme tiers, extending Cook-Sather et al. (2014) beyond reflective feedback into structural curriculum governance. Introduce caption-enabled live lectures as standard provision. Extend the accessibility panel to include cognitive load reduction tools. Share this ePortfolio with departmental colleagues as a catalyst for faculty-wide adoption of UDL, aligned with ATU’s strategic inclusion commitments and SDG 4.

Designing the Interactive AI Learning Dashboard required me to dismantle assumptions I had not previously questioned that students could read dense slides at speed, that lab access was sufficient, that assessment anxiety reflected individual deficit rather than design, and that accessibility was an add-on rather than a precondition of effective teaching. Mezirow (1991) conceptualises this form of perspective transformation as the deepest level of adult learning: not the acquisition of new knowledge, but the reconstitution of the frame through which knowledge is interpreted. The value of human diversity encompassing varied talents, perspectives, linguistic resources, cognitive styles, and lived experiences is not a challenge this philosophy accommodates, but the condition upon which it is built. Every design decision between September 2025 and April 2026 was shaped by a single question: how can a learning environment be structured so that human diversity functions as a resource rather than a constraint? The evidence is present in every accessible module, every submitted reflection, and every student who reported, for the first time, a sense of belonging in an AI learning environment.
