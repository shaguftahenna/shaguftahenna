Evaluation & Effectiveness

The effectiveness of the UDL redesign is evaluated through two complementary strands of evidence. Part 1 presents qualitative findings derived from 186 student reflections across eight modules, collected using two structured prompts per session. Part 2 provides quantitative evidence from a post-intervention survey (n=15), administered in March 2026, comprising 18 Likert-scale items across five evaluative domains. Together, these strands constitute a mixed-methods evaluation that establishes a clear link between design implementation and measurable inclusive learning outcomes. This design is informed by Stufflebeam's (1971) CIPP (Context, Input, Process, Product) evaluation model, whereby qualitative reflections generate process-level insights into learner engagement, while survey data provide product-level evidence of outcome effectiveness.

Part 1 — Qualitative Evidence Base Student reflections · Academic literature · Thematic analysis

Student Reflections & Academic Evidence

A total of 186 student reflections were submitted across 8 evaluated AI modules using two guided prompts per module session. Prompt 1 asked students what they observed in the simulation; Prompt 2 asked how they would apply the concept to a real-world problem. Reflections were analysed qualitatively against UDL principles, Kolb's (1984) experiential cycle, and Moon's (2004) depth taxonomy. This evidence provides the qualitative foundation for the quantitative outcomes measured in Part 2.

The eight modules span three tiers: AI Foundations (Graph Theory, Minimax), Deep Learning (RNNs, Autoencoders, CNNs), and Advanced Concepts (GANs, Diffusion Models, Self-Supervised Learning). With two reflections per module per student, the maximum possible output across a cohort of 15 would be 240 submissions. The observed 186 submissions represent a 77.5% engagement rate, significantly above the pre-intervention baseline of 0% structured reflective engagement.

Reflection & Self-Regulation

Structured reflection transforms experience into learning. Boud, Keogh and Walker (1985) and Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) establish guided prompts as the mechanism that converts simulation engagement into self-regulated conceptual understanding.

Boud et al. (1985) · Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) · Panadero (2017)
Student Voice as Co-Design

Student voice treated as a co-design resource rather than a satisfaction metric produces inclusive improvement with stronger pedagogical validity. Moon (2004) identifies reflection depth as the primary indicator of meaningful deep learning beyond surface recall.

Cook-Sather et al. (2014) · Moon (2004) · Bond et al. (2020)
Applied to This Dashboard

The two-prompt reflection system operationalises all three principles simultaneously: embedding formative assessment into every module page, making student voice structurally visible, and producing the 77.5% engagement rate evidenced in this section.

Marking criteria — directly addressed

Cook-Sather et al. (2014) argue that inclusive improvement is reliable when it is co-designed with student voice. The four changes below were directly identified by students in their reflections as the most beneficial inclusive developments to the AI Learning Dashboard. Each change is grounded in peer-reviewed literature, documented as implemented or in progress, and validated by the post-intervention results in Section 05, which confirm that the changes produced measurable improvements in engagement, accessibility, and learning effectiveness.

Cook-Sather et al. (2014) Student voice as co-design Evidence-based inclusive change Voice-to-action documented
Multi-language selector (EN / ES / FR)
Frequently cited
"I used language translations alongside the English notes, English is my second language and I had never had that option before in any computing course." — M. L., March 2026

Students for whom English is an additional language identified language support as the single highest-barrier removal in the AI module set. The multi-language selector was added to the accessibility panel directly in response to this cohort feedback. This operationalises Kieran and Anderson (2018) argument that UDL must address linguistic diversity.

Kieran and Anderson (2018) — UDL and linguistic equity
Implemented: March 2025 dashboard update
Animated GIF preview at dashboard card
Frequently cited
"The animated GIF of A* was the first time pathfinding genuinely made sense to me. I watched it loop three times before reading the lecture notes." — J. K., March 2026

Students in AI Foundations identified that encountering a visual preview of the algorithm before opening the module page lowered the cognitive threshold for engagement. An animated GIF walkthrough was added to the A* dashboard card in direct response. This reflects Mayer's (2009) dual-channel principle: visual pre-exposure reduces extraneous cognitive load when formal content is encountered.

Mayer (2009) — multimedia learning, dual-channel processing
Implemented: A* module, January 2025
Always-visible Help & Compliance menu
Multiple cohorts
"Having a Help & Compliance link always visible meant I never felt lost when the content got genuinely difficult." — R. K., March 2026

Students reported that in advanced modules such as GANs, the absence of a persistent support link created disengagement when content difficulty peaked. The Help & Compliance menu was expanded with per-tier guidance links, creating a consistent, always-accessible safety net. Bransford, Brown and Cocking (1999) identify structured support environments as essential to maintaining learning momentum.

Bransford, Brown and Cocking (1999) — structured support environments
Implemented: Phase 5, March 2026
Read-aloud with persistence across module pages
EAL and disability-identified
"The read-aloud feature meant I could listen to the Diffusion Models notes during my commute. This module felt designed for real-world experience." — Ch. D., March 2026

Students with dyslexia and those studying outside conventional settings identified the Web Speech API read-aloud as transformative for equitable access. Student feedback requested the feature persist across page navigation, a development currently in progress for 2026. Rose and Meyer (2002) emphasise that accessibility features built from the start, not retrofitted.

Rose and Meyer (2002); CAST (2024) multiple means of representation
In progress: persistent read-aloud, 2025–26
