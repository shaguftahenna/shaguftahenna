Iterative UDL Design · September 2025 to April 2026

Implementation of Change

From initial accessibility audit to fully deployed AI learning dashboard, the redesign was undertaken as a structured, iterative process across five phases. Each phase operationalised CAST UDL 3.0 at the level of system architecture, ensuring that decisions relating to content, interaction, and assessment were informed by principles of Engagement, Representation, and Action and Expression from the outset. The redevelopment of all 18 modules was guided by UDL guidelines, grounded in established pedagogical theory, and validated through post-intervention data.

Theoretical and Curriculum Design Framing · UDL as Architectural Constraint, Not Retrofit

The implementation of UDL across the Interactive AI Learning Dashboard was not approached as a series of accessibility adjustments appended to an existing module. It was undertaken as a fundamental curriculum redesign, guided by CAST's (2024) principles. This position is consistent with Tyler's (1949) foundational curriculum design principle that objectives, learning experiences, organisation, and evaluation must be coherently aligned before instruction begins. Rose and Meyer (2002) extend this argument into inclusive design: the curriculum itself, when designed without flexibility, is the primary barrier to learning, and only proactive architectural redesign removes that barrier meaningfully.

The five implementation phases follow a trajectory that mirrors Laurillard's (2012) conversational framework: audit and diagnosis, design and build, iterative deployment, reflective evidence-gathering, and responsive refinement. This maps closely to Kelly's (2004) argument that curriculum development is not a linear delivery process but a cyclical, evidence-driven practice of design, enactment, and critical evaluation. Each phase documented here includes its UDL guideline mapping, theoretical justification, and post-intervention outcome, closing the loop between student diagnostic voice and measurable pedagogical change.

Curriculum Design Principle

UDL embedded as an architectural constraint from the first module page built: every subsequent page inherits the same inclusive foundation regardless of tier (Tyler, 1949; Rose and Meyer, 2002).

Evidence Base

8 pre-intervention student reflections across 3 tiers identified 4 convergent structural barriers: the diagnostic brief for every implementation decision (Cook-Sather, Bovill and Felten (2014); Brookfield(2017).

Validated Outcomes

Post-intervention survey (Section 05) confirms every phase produced its intended inclusive outcome, closing the loop between student voice and measurable pedagogical change (Biggs and Tang, 2011).

September 2025 · Phase 1
UDL Audit of Existing AI Module

A systematic accessibility and curriculum inclusion audit of the AI module was conducted against CAST UDL Guidelines 3.0 across all nine guidelines and 31 checkpoints. The audit examined all 18 existing modules, assessing each for representation flexibility, engagement scaffolding, and expression pathways. Twenty-one distinct barriers were identified, clustered around five structural conditions: single-format content delivery with no video, simulation, or written summary; absence of any search or filter system; no persistent accessibility controls; no structured reflection mechanism to support conceptual consolidation; and inaccessible navigation excluding keyboard and screen-reader users. This is a curriculum diagnosis in the tradition of Tyler's (1949) objectives-first model, where gaps between intended and enacted learning are the starting point for principled redesign.

Cook-Sather, Bovill and Felten (2014) establish that authentic curriculum change must begin with evidence. The pre-intervention reflections in Section 02 provided the qualitative dimension: eight students across three tiers independently naming the same structural gaps. Brookfield (2017) identifies this convergence as the most reliable signal of systemic curriculum failure. Thijs and van den Akker (2009) describe evidence-informed curriculum development as essential to sustainable inclusive design: without systematic diagnosis mapped to theoretical frameworks, redesign is merely cosmetic adjustment.

October 2025 · Phase 2
Dashboard Design & Multi-Modal Content

The Interactive AI Learning Dashboard was rebuilt from scratch as a UDL-embedded learning environment. Every design decision was treated as a curriculum constraint: all 18 module pages would inherit the same inclusive foundation before any content was written. The persistent accessibility panel was built into the base HTML template first, operationalising Rose and Meyer's (2002) principle that accessible design produces qualitatively different access when built in from the outset. The curriculum architecture reflects Biggs and Tang's (2011) constructive alignment model: outcomes, content modes, and assessment were designed as a coherent system, not assembled incrementally.

February 2026 · Phase 3
Interactive Simulation Deployment

Live interactive simulations were deployed across all 18 modules at the start of Semester 2, converting the dashboard from a multimodal content repository into a simulation-first learning environment. All simulations are keyboard-navigable, screen-reader compatible, WCAG 2.1 AA compliant, and accessible asynchronously, removing the lab-only constraint students identified as the most significant structural barrier. De Jong et al. (2023) provide empirical evidence that inquiry-based environments combining direct instruction with simulation produce measurably stronger conceptual outcomes. The sequencing decision to place simulation before formal theory operationalises Kolb's (1984) experiential cycle: concrete experience precedes reflective observation, which precedes abstract conceptualisation.

March 2026 · Phase 4
Reflection System & Progress Tracking Rollout

The localStorage reflection system was rolled out uniformly across all 18 modules, establishing the formative assessment architecture. Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) identify guided prompts as one of the seven principles of effective formative assessment practice: this system operationalises their model at the architectural level, embedding the feedback mechanism directly into each module page rather than routing students to a separate platform. Ramsden (2003) argues that assessment is the most powerful signal in a learning environment: when students see reflection structurally integrated into every module, their orientation shifts from content consumption to active conceptual engagement.

Reflection System Implementation · All Modules
Student Identity Persistence
section.student-details with name and optional ID input, saved via saveStudentDetails(). Student ID derived from name if not provided. Enables per-student localStorage keying across sessions for each module.
Two Guided Reflection Prompts per Module · Specific Examples Across Tiers
Prompt 1: "What did you observe in the simulation?" targets Kolb's (1984) Stage 2 (reflective observation). Prompt 2: "How would you apply what you have learned to a real-world problem?" targets Stage 3 and Stage 4 (abstract conceptualisation, active experimentation) and Moon's (2004) analytical depth. Each textarea calls submitReflection(id), storing content and timestamp in localStorage, then disabling and greying the textarea to signal submission. The UDL rationale for two structured prompts rather than one open field is that scaffolding reduces the blank-page anxiety that Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) identify as the most common barrier to voluntary reflective engagement.
Minimax Algorithm · Tier 1 · MODULE_NAME = "Minimax"
Prompt 1 asks what the student observed about game tree evaluation and decision paths in the Minimax simulator. Prompt 2 asks how they would apply minimax decision-making to a real-world AI problem such as adversarial search or game-playing agents. Tier 1 students consistently reported that the simulation made the concept of minimising the maximum possible loss tangible before the abstract game tree notation was introduced, operationalising Mezirow's (1997) principle of reducing disorienting dilemmas at the point of conceptual entry.
RNNs · Tier 2 · MODULE_NAME = "RNNs"
Prompt 1 asks what the student observed about hidden state changes across time steps in the sequential data simulator. Prompt 2 asks how they would apply RNNs to speech recognition or time-series prediction. Reflections are persistent across sessions and device restarts, ensuring no student loses progress.
Diffusion Models · Tier 3 · MODULE_NAME = "Diffusion"
Prompt 1 asks what the student observed during the forward and reverse diffusion process in the Diffusion Explainer simulation. Prompt 2 asks how diffusion-based generation could be applied to creative AI, medical imaging, or data augmentation. The Diffusion module generated the highest-quality reflections in the post-intervention dataset: students consistently moved from descriptive observation into analytical engagement with stochastic process limitations, precisely the transformative depth Moon (2004) identifies as the target of structured reflective practice.
Real-Time Progress Bar
div.progress-bar#progressBar updates width and text dynamically via updateProgress() on each reflection submission. CSS transition provides smooth visual feedback. Operationalises GL 8.4 (progress monitoring and self-regulation): students see exactly where they are in the module at all times, reducing completion anxiety. 79% of students submitted at least one reflection in their first week.

April 2026 · Phase 5
Iterative Feedback & Responsive Curriculum Refinement

A mid-semester survey was deployed across all three module tiers in response to the first wave of post-intervention reflection data. Kelly (2004) argues that curriculum development is never complete at deployment: it is an ongoing process of critical evaluation and responsive adaptation. Boud and Molloy (2013) extend this into feedback practice: sustainable feedback models must build learner capacity to self-assess and feed forward. Three further adaptations were made in direct response to student voice data, each addressing a residual gap the cohort identified.

Integrated Implementation Summary · UDL as Iterative Curriculum Design Practice

A Structurally Different Learning Environment

The five implementation phases collectively constitute a model of UDL as iterative curriculum design practice rather than one-time compliance. Each phase was initiated by evidence: pre-intervention student reflections, audit findings, and each was validated by measurable post-intervention outcomes documented in Section 05. This cycle of diagnosis, design, deployment, and responsive refinement is the operationalisation of Laurillard's (2012) conversational framework applied at the module architecture level, and it reflects Kelly's (2004) characterisation of curriculum development as a continuous, evidence-responsive process rather than a fixed product.

What distinguishes this implementation from a standard accessibility audit is that the evidence trail runs in both directions: from student voice to design decision, and from design decision back to student outcome. Every change is traceable to a specific learner-identified barrier, and every barrier is traceable to a CAST UDL checkpoint. The result is not a more accessible version of the original module. It is a structurally different learning environment in which variability in learners was anticipated from the first line of code, embodying the inclusive curriculum design principles that Tyler (1949), Rose and Meyer (2002), and CAST (2024) each identify as essential conditions for equitable learning.

Iterative Cycle

Audit, Design, Deploy, Evaluate, Refine: mirroring Laurillard's (2012) conversational framework and Kelly's (2004) curriculum as process model across five discrete phases over eight months.

Student Voice

Every implementation decision is directly traceable to a specific student-identified barrier: Cook-Sather et al.'s (2014) evidence-based partnership model applied in live curriculum practice.

Validated Outcomes

94% simulation uptake, +28% satisfaction, 186 reflections submitted: each metric closes the loop between design intent and measurable inclusive learning outcome (Section 05).

Accessibility Implementation · CAST Checkpoints 1.1 to 1.5 · Persistent Panel on All 18 Modules

Access as Infrastructure

The persistent accessibility panel is not a feature of the dashboard: it is the dashboard's foundational architectural layer. Built into the base HTML template before any module content was written, the panel ensures that every learner who opens any of the 18 module pages encounters the same five access controls without navigating away from their learning content. Rose and Meyer (2002) describe this as the essential distinction between accessible design and inclusive design: accessibility ensures entry; inclusive design ensures engagement. The five controls directly operationalise CAST Checkpoints 1.1 to 1.5, removing perceptual and linguistic barriers at the structural level.

Blaschke (2012) identifies self-determined learnings as dependent on learner control over the conditions of engagement: when students can adjust font size, contrast, or language without institutional permission, they enact the autonomy that heutagogical approaches identify as essential for deep learning. This is particularly significant in an AI module where the cognitive demands of notation, algorithmic abstraction, and mathematical formalism are already high: removing perceptual barriers reduces the extraneous cognitive load that would otherwise compete with germane load required for conceptual understanding (Mayer, 2009).

Five Accessibility Controls · div.accessibility-panel
All 18 modules
Font size controls (A+ / A−) CP 1.1
increaseFont() / decreaseFont() via CSS class injection. No page reload. Persistent across session.
High contrast toggle CP 1.2
toggleHighContrast() applies body[data-theme="high-contrast"]. WCAG 4.5:1 contrast ratio maintained throughout.
Dyslexia font (OpenDyslexic) CP 1.3
toggleDyslexiaFont() swaps font-family to OpenDyslexic across all module text. Reduces visual crowding.
Read aloud (Web Speech API) CP 1.4
readAloud() uses SpeechSynthesis API to convert visible module text to speech. Supports auditory learners.
Language selector EN / ES / FR CP 1.5
changeLanguage(value) via select#languageSelect onchange. Interface translates without page reload. Kieran and Anderson (2018).

Policy and Legal Compliance · UDL as Compliance Architecture · WCAG 2.1 AA · GDPR · EU AI Act

Compliance by Design

The Interactive AI Learning Dashboard treats policy compliance not as a checklist but as a curriculum design commitment embedded into the system architecture. The EU Web Accessibility Directive (2016) and the Irish Disability Act (2005) require that digital education materials are accessible to all learners including those registered with disability services. Rather than addressing these requirements through reactive accommodation, the dashboard operationalises them through the UDL accessibility panel built into every module page before content was written. This approach reflects the principle that inclusive design is the most legally robust form of compliance: when access is structural, no learner needs to disclose a disability to receive equitable participation.

GDPR compliance is addressed through privacy by design: the dashboard uses localStorage for reflection persistence, meaning student data remains on the learner's own device rather than being transmitted to external servers. No personal identifiers are collected beyond name and optional student ID entered voluntarily for reflection keying. The EU AI Act (2024) classifies educational AI tools as low-risk but imposes transparency obligations: every simulation embedded in the dashboard carries a descriptive title attribute, a written conceptual summary, and lecture content explaining the algorithm's behaviour, ensuring learners understand the AI system they are engaging with before and after interaction.

Digital Accessibility · WCAG 2.1 AA

All iframes carry descriptive title attributes. Keyboard navigation exits all simulation frames cleanly. Font scaling, contrast, and dyslexia font controls meet WCAG 1.4 and 1.4.3 requirements. EU Web Accessibility Directive (2016) and Irish Disability Act (2005) compliance embedded by design.

Data Protection · GDPR Privacy by Design

Reflections stored in localStorage on the learner's device. No server-side collection. Student ID optional and derived from name only. Retention controlled by learner. Aligns with GDPR Article 5 data minimisation and purpose limitation principles.

AI Transparency · EU AI Act (2024)

Every simulation carries a title attribute, written conceptual summary, and lecture explanation of algorithm behaviour. Educational AI classified as low-risk under EU AI Act. Transparency obligations embedded architecturally, not appended as disclaimers.

Inclusive Education Rights · UN CRPD · UNESCO 2030

UN CRPD: access to technology-enhanced learning without discrimination. UNESCO Education 2030 equity goals: language selector (EN/ES/FR) ensures linguistic inclusion. OECD AI Principles: transparency and fairness built into all interactive simulations.

Compliance as UDL Practice · Theoretical Grounding · Further detail: help.html#udl and udl_law.html
The dashboard demonstrates that UDL implementation and legal compliance are not parallel obligations but the same architectural commitment expressed in different registers. When access is built into the system by design, legal requirements are met not through accommodation workflows but through the learning environment itself. This reflects Biesta's (2009) argument that good education requires coherent alignment of purpose, process, and accountability: the compliance framework is the pedagogical framework, operationalised in code.
