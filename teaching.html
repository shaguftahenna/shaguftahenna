Teaching Philosophy Statement

Empowering Every Learner to Think, Create, and Solve

01Philosophy & foundations

My teaching philosophy is grounded in an integrated synthesis of cognitive science, socio-constructivism, and inclusive curriculum design. I conceptualise learning as a process of sustained transformation in understanding, capability, and independence, achieved through structured engagement, guided interaction, and progressive autonomy. Learning, in this sense, is not the acquisition of information but the development of the capacity to apply, transfer, and critically evaluate knowledge across contexts.

02Theoretical grounding

Drawing on Bransford et al. (1999), I understand learning as most effective when new knowledge is actively connected to prior understanding within structured environments. Vygotsky's (1978) Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) positions learning as socially mediated, requiring guided support to extend capability, while Bruner (1961) emphasises scaffolding as a mechanism for progressively transferring responsibility to the learner. I therefore conceptualise learning as an active, socially situated, and scaffolded process in which knowledge is constructed through experience, reflection, and interaction. Teaching, within this framework, is the intentional design of conditions that enable this construction, rather than the transmission of content.

03Design & practice

I approach AI education as a systems design problem in which cognition, accessibility, interaction, and assessment are engineered as an integrated learning environment. This is operationalised through my Interactive AI Learning Dashboard, which translates theory into practice through multimodal design, combining simulations, video, lectures, and interactive tasks. Within this environment, responsibility for learning is shared but progressively transferred: I design structured, scaffolded pathways, while learners actively engage, reflect, and assume increasing independence. Learning is evidenced not through content recall, but through demonstrated application, articulation, and transfer of knowledge, supported by embedded formative reflection and iterative feedback.

04Inclusivity as principle

Inclusivity is embedded as a foundational design principle rather than an additional consideration. Guided by CAST and the Universal Design for Learning (UDL) framework, I design for learner variability through multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression. This ensures that all learners are positioned as capable participants in knowledge construction, with equitable access to the conditions required for meaningful learning.

Learning is a shared responsibility between the educator, who designs structured and inclusive learning environments, and the learner, who actively engages with, reflects on, and applies knowledge within those environments. Guided by CAST (2024), I treat variability in learners not as deviation from a norm but as the foundation for design.

While this integrated approach strengthens inclusivity, engagement, and cognitive accessibility, it also requires careful instructional design to manage cognitive load, ensuring that multimodal richness supports rather than overwhelms learners.

The response

This concern is addressed through Universal Design for Learning, which provides a structured framework for designing learning environments that account for learner variability while maintaining cognitive coherence.

Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle in AI Education

Active and experiential learning is a cornerstone of my practice. In every AI module, this cycle is explicit, students experience phenomenon first, then reflect, then build theory, then apply.

🔴Stage 1 — Concrete ExperienceEntry point▼
Students open the GANLab simulation or an A* grid visualiser and interact with it directly, before reading any notes or watching any lecture. The AI concept is encountered as a phenomenon: observable, manipulable, surprising. This entry point equalises prior knowledge differences, what a student already knows about neural networks is less important than what they can observe happening in front of them.
Kolb (1984) · Prince (2004)
🟡Stage 2 — Reflective ObservationGuided▼
Reflection Prompt 1: "What did you observe in the simulation? What surprised you? What did you expect that didn't happen?", structured to guide observation rather than summarise content.
Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006)
🔵Stage 3 — Abstract ConceptualisationMulti-modal▼

The always-visible Help & Compliance menu, structured module layout, and persistent progress bar are not merely UX decisions, they are cognitive load management strategies. Students with mathematics anxiety, English as an additional language, or non-conventional study schedules experience AI content in a predictable, reassuring environment where the path forward is always visible and support is never more than one click away.

Multimodal delivery at this stage is deliberate, different representations serve different cognitive needs, and no single format is privileged as the correct way to understand the content.
Mayer (2009) · Bransford et al. (1999)
🟢Stage 4 — Active ExperimentationEvidence▼
Reflection Prompt 2: "How would you apply what you have learned? Where else might this algorithm or architecture be useful?", transferring understanding to new contexts and developing critical thinking.
Kolb (1984) · Prince (2004)

Assessment as an Act of Inclusion

Assessment is integral to inclusivity. I prioritise formative, low-stakes assessments, reflective exercises, peer review, and iterative projects, that reduce anxiety while promoting self-regulation and metacognitive skills. Learning is evidenced through learners’ ability to explain concepts, apply them in novel contexts, and demonstrate increasing independence in problem-solving and reflection. Assessment is therefore designed as an integrated evidence system combining reflection, application, and engagement data rather than a single summative measure.

✍️Structured In-Browser Reflections18 modules▼
Two guided reflections per module, stored in browser localStorage per student name and ID. Timestamped on submission, textarea locked to prevent overwriting. 186 reflections submitted in February–March 2026. Low-stakes by design.
Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) · CAST (2024)
🎯Real-Time Progress VisibilityStudent-facing▼
Every module page includes a real-time progress bar (0% → 100%) that updates as students complete reflections. Students always see exactly where they are in the module, reducing anxiety and supporting self-regulation.
Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006)
🔄Iterative, Adaptive Module DesignEvidence-driven▼
Mid-semester surveys, reflection quality analysis, and engagement data inform module refinement. Adaptations made in 2026 in direct response to student data: Help menu expanded, multi-language selector added, A* GIF preview piloted.
Prince (2004) · CAST (2024)
🌈Flexible Demonstration of UnderstandingUDL aligned▼
Written reflection, simulation-based observation, peer-to-peer comparison, and iterative project work all sit alongside each other. No single assessment mode is privileged, particularly important where mathematics anxiety can mask genuine conceptual understanding.
CAST (2024) · Rose and Meyer (2002)

Continuous Reflection & Responsiveness

I actively seek student feedback, evaluate the impact of teaching strategies, and refine my practice based on evidence and emerging best practices. Inclusivity is therefore dynamic, evolving with research, technology, and student diversity. The addition of the multi-language selector and animated GIF previews in 2025 are direct examples of practice changing in response to what students identified as missing.

CAST (2024)Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006)
🌱
Growth Mindset & Lifelong Learning

Reflection and self-evaluation are embedded throughout all 18 AI modules. Students who engage with the reflection system are building metacognitive skills that extend far beyond AI content. The ability to articulate what you observed, what you understood, and how you would apply it is a transferable academic skill, and one that the structured two-prompt system is designed to develop consistently across every topic.

Bransford et al. (1999)Kolb (1984)

My approach to inclusive teaching is grounded in theory, research, and sustained reflective practice. Designing the AI Learning Dashboard required me to critically examine assumptions I had not previously questioned that students could read dense slides at speed, that lab access was sufficient, that assessment anxiety was an individual problem rather than a design failure. Each of those assumptions, once named, became a design decision. By building multi-modal, accessible, and interactive learning experiences from the first line of code, I aim to ensure that all students can participate fully and achieve their highest potential. Inclusive education is not a professional add-on, it is the condition under which genuine learning becomes possible for every learner. In this model, I position myself not only as a lecturer but as a learning architect, responsible for designing environments in which all students can succeed.
